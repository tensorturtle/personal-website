# The Gospel for Those Who Didn't Enjoy the Military

*A book I am writing. Early — this is the premise, not the manuscript.*

## Who it is for

Christian writing borrows heavily from the military. Soldier of Christ. The armor of
God. Marching orders, the good fight, standing your post. The metaphors are old and
they are scriptural, and they tend to be handled by people for whom the association is
pleasant — who found in service a brotherhood, a proving, a story they are glad to
tell.

I was conscripted. I did not find it inspiring. And for people like me the whole
vocabulary lands wrong: told to be a good soldier by a faith that is supposed to be
about being loved, the image does not lift, it presses down. So the metaphors get
quietly skipped, and with them the passages they came from.

This book is for the people doing the skipping. The premise is that they are the ones
best placed to understand what the passages actually say — not despite having disliked
it, but because of it.

## The turn

The military is not an analogy for the kingdom of heaven because both involve
obedience. It is an analogy because it shows you, in the body, what submission to
authority *costs* — and therefore exactly what would have to be true of an authority
for that cost to be worth paying.

If you enjoyed your service, the analogy flatters and you learn nothing from it. If you
did not, it does something better: it gives you a precise, non-theoretical account of
what makes authority unbearable. Orders whose reason you are not entitled to know. A
rank that has nothing to do with worth. Being managed rather than known. Whoever can
name why that was intolerable is holding a very exact description, in the negative, of
the thing the gospel claims God is.

That is the book's one move, applied repeatedly. Not *the military was secretly good*.
Closer to: every place it went wrong is a place where scripture claims something goes
right, and having felt the wrong version is what lets you recognize the difference.

## The chapters, roughly

**You did not choose to be here.** Conscription and calling are both received rather
than selected. The difference is not whether you were consulted. It is who is on the
other end.

**A rank you did not earn.** In the army, status arrives without reference to what you
are worth, and everyone knows it. Imputed righteousness is the same structure —
standing conferred rather than achieved — and the reason one is an insult and the other
is good news is entirely a question of the giver.

**Issued, not chosen.** The uniform makes identity something put on you rather than
expressed by you. Being clothed in Christ is that grammar exactly, and it is the part
that most offends anyone who thinks identity is self-authored.

**Hurry up and wait.** Most of service is waiting for orders that are not coming yet.
There is a version of waiting that hollows you out and a version that forms you, and
the difference is not the duration.

**The centurion.** The person in the Gospels who understood Jesus' authority fastest
was a career military officer, and he understood it *because* he was under command
himself. Matthew 8. This is the hinge chapter.

**The officer who goes first.** What was actually missing was not discipline or
hardship. It was a commander who takes the worst position rather than assigns it. The
claim of the incarnation is that this is precisely what happened, and it is the one
thing the institution I served could not produce.

**Discharge.** Every conscript's calendar is oriented toward a date that ends it.
Living toward a promised end is not escapism; it is the only reason the middle is
survivable. Christian hope has the same shape and a better date.

## What I want to avoid

Two failure modes, both easy.

The first is redeeming it too fast — the memoir where the hard thing turns out to have
been a gift all along, and every injustice gets retroactively assigned a purpose. Some
of what happens under conscription is simply wrong, and saying so is not a lack of
faith. A book that cannot say a thing was wrong cannot be trusted when it says a thing
was good.

The second is the opposite: grievance with scripture stapled on. I am close enough to
this material that the temptation is real.

The test I want to hold it to is whether someone who resents their service could read
it without feeling either corrected or flattered. That is a narrow gap, and I do not
know yet whether I can write in it.
